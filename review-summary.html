Protecting the Brain
While Treating the Infection

A narrative review of therapies for primary amoebic meningoencephalitis (PAM), a rare and almost always fatal brain infection, and of how the brain’s own response could be addressed alongside treatment of the infection.

Yuval Gal-Shahaf | Narrative review | Unpublished manuscript

The Problem

A rare disease with few options

PAM is caused by Naegleria fowleri, an amoeba that can enter through the nose during freshwater swimming and travel to the brain. The illness progresses within days, and it is often mistaken for common viral or bacterial meningitis, which delays diagnosis.

The review cites survival of under 3% with the current standard approach, with only a handful of known survivors among roughly 400 reported cases. Because the disease is so rare, there are no human trials to guide better treatment.

The Idea

Two fronts, not one

Current treatment aims at the amoeba. The review asks whether that is enough. Once the amoeba reaches the brain, the brain’s own response becomes part of the damage: immune cells are destroyed, inflammation rises, and oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction keep feeding each other. The drugs themselves can also burden the nervous system.

Front 1: the infection

Examine the drugs used in survivors, the evidence behind them, and newer candidates and combinations identified in laboratory studies.

Front 2: the brain

Map how neuroinflammation and oxidative stress cause lasting injury, and which protective strategies from neuroscience could reduce it.

What the Review Covers

From drug evidence to brain biology

The current approach, examined

What survivors received, and a critical look at each component, including evidence that one commonly used drug may add little and interfere with another.

Emerging candidates

Newer drugs and combinations that showed synergy in laboratory studies, with their mechanisms explained.

The brain’s response

How the amoeba damages microglia, the brain’s immune cells, and triggers a vicious cycle of inflammation and oxidative stress.

Protective strategies

Ideas drawn from neuroscience to calm neuroinflammation, limit oxidative damage and support brain resilience, including the role of BDNF.

An Open Question

Why mostly young males?

PAM is reported more often in young males. The usual explanation is behavior, such as time spent in warm freshwater. The review raises a further possibility: that hormonal factors, such as the neuroprotective effects reported for estrogen and progesterone, may also play a role, and suggests this deserves research.

Context and Limits

What this is, and what it is not

The review was written voluntarily to support a medical team treating a patient. It brings together published laboratory and clinical evidence from several fields, which is the same approach behind the model in the post-COVID work.

Status: this is a narrative review and a source of hypotheses. It is not clinical guidance or medical advice, and the combinations it discusses have not been tested in human trials.
